Specs, from idea to launch
  • AI Visibility Audit API — idea to launch to measured use. A public product, launched July 2026: anyone pastes a website address and, with no login, gets a score for how well ChatGPT, Perplexity and Claude can find and cite it. I designed the rubric, 34 checks in 4 weighted categories, and an output a non-technical buyer can act on: a grade, and the fix and the reason for every failed check. 420+ audits across 210+ sites, most of them run by my own outreach agents before a new prospect is contacted; in the logs, 48 audits of 16 different sites came from 16 addresses that are not my laptop or our server.
  • My acceptance criteria are tests: a change is accepted only when the tests and the production logs prove it works. 600+ automated tests across unit, integration and evaluation layers.
  • The spec says what the model decides and what code decides: hard facts in a posting, such as a country restriction or a pay figure, are checked in code; judgment calls go to the model. When one of my override rules wrongly let three postings through because it saw "LATAM" and ignored "U.S. only", I changed it to ask the same location checker the filters use.
Roadmap — what gets built, and what gets cut
  • Prioritized from outcome data. In my job-search pipeline, outcome labels showed one role category made up 7 of the 12 postings I rejected but only 2 of the 12 I rated positive. I cut it from targeting within a day and re-pointed the searches to the categories that fit.
  • Said no on evidence. Two job boards looked equally good on their websites, but one had no posting newer than 33 days and 91% past their own expiry. I rejected it, connected the other, and wrote down why.
  • Decided under uncertainty. When a vendor retired the AI models my services used, I moved all six products onto a five-provider fallback chain that week, with no outage.
Production data → the problems worth solving
  • Trial sign-ups to EspaLuz, my language-tutor bot, had never reached the CRM since launch, while the sender's log looked healthy. I found it by auditing from the receiving end, fixed it and backfilled all 28 lost users.
  • Built a reply detector so a prospect's answer is not lost in the inbox: it reads two inboxes and matches every sender against the CRM. Replayed on 60 days of real mail, it narrowed 2,432 emails to the 73 from people linked to a deal, including a prospect's reply that had sat unseen for nearly eight weeks. Live, it checks every 10 minutes and alerts me with the deal linked.
Outcome measurement and iteration
  • An upgrade to my AI screening step (retrieval, RAG) scored lower on a test set built from my own past decisions, so it stayed switched off: a change that measures worse does not ship, however sophisticated.
  • A new job source cleared my live filter 72% of the time, across all 345 of its postings, so it stayed.
  • I fix the measurement itself: my screening replay was grading against blank postings, so its score measured the blank. Each decision is now stored with its real posting and replayed before a change ships.
  • "It ran" is not "it worked": one search path ran on schedule with its filters silently switched off. The first fixed run rejected five junk results that used to become CRM deals.

How I Run a Product

  1. Find the problem in the data: where money or time is lost, read from production logs, the CRM and the receiving end.
  2. Decide and sequence: bottleneck first; say no, in writing, to what the evidence does not support.
  3. Spec it with acceptance tests: hard rules in code, judgment calls to the model, a human approval where a mistake is costly.
  4. Ship the smallest change that tests the fix.
  5. Measure after launch and iterate: harden what works; switch off what measures worse, even a technically impressive change.

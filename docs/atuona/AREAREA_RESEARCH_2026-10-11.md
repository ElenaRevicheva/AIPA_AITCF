Elena: "search for Arearea history of painting … dive deeply into the history of art first". Facts below are from the sources
listed; anything a source did not support is marked as such.

## The painting

- **Arearea**, Paul Gauguin, **1892**, oil on canvas, 75 × 93 cm (Artchive gives 75 × 94). Painted in Tahiti during his first stay
  (he arrived April 1891). Musée d'Orsay, Paris (RF 1961 6). Alternate title **"Joyeusetés"**; the Tahitian word is glossed as
  "joyeuseté / divertissement" (amusement, joyfulness). Its popular name was always **"le Chien rouge"** — the Red Dog (Larousse;
  a 1906 Vollard invoice to the Prince de Wagram lists it as "chien rouge"; a 1900 Vollard receipt describes it as "Chien rouge
  et 2 femmes accroupies, jouant de la flûte (?)").
- **What is in it** (checked against the Orsay image itself, Wikimedia Commons, public domain): two women seated on dark green
  ground — the one on the left in a blue pareu, bare-breasted, eyes down, playing a small flute; the one on the right in a white
  one-shoulder dress, looking straight out. A blue-grey trunk with white blossoms cuts the canvas top to bottom. A red-orange dog,
  head down, sniffs the ground in the lower left — no shadow. Far behind, small women before a tall idol. No sky: flat bands of
  green, vermilion and yellow.
- **The idol and the rite are invented:** Gauguin enlarged a small Maori figure to the size of a great Buddha and invented a
  sacred ceremony around it (Musée d'Orsay text, via Google Arts & Culture and Ciné-club de Caen).
- The Orsay calls it a picture where "dream and reality coexist": imagined scenes mixed with what he saw, local stories and old
  religion (Google Arts & Culture).

## Its history

- **November 1893, Galerie Durand-Ruel, Paris** — shown with the Tahitian paintings (catalogue no. 8). The reception was not the
  enthusiasm he hoped for: the Tahitian titles irritated many of his friends, and **the red dog provoked much sarcasm**.
- A French source reports that at a later showing an English visitor cried out in horror: "Red dog!"
- **1895, Hôtel Drouot sale** — Gauguin **bought it back himself** before leaving Europe for good; he considered it one of his
  best canvases.
- Later: the Lung couple, Algiers → **bequeathed to the French state, 1961** → Louvre → **Musée d'Orsay since 1986**.
- Not to be confused with **Arearea no varua ino** (1894, Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek, Copenhagen — "the amusement of the evil spirit"),
  and the 1894 monotype at the National Gallery of Art; the Degas ownership found online belongs to those, not to the Orsay
  painting.
- Not verified: a Gauguin letter or interview line about the red dog itself (his 13 May 1895 interview with Eugène Tardieu in
  L'Écho de Paris exists; no source quoted a dog remark from it). Nothing found tying the idol to Hina.

## Her own content

- **Fragment #087 "Red Dog"** in the vault is about Arearea: "Two women — in the foreground. / No gesture. No address. / Behind —
  a flute. / Further still — an idol. / Not god. / The image of god. / And between all this — / a red dog. / Fact: dogs aren't
  red. / Fact: it has no shadow. / Fact: Gauguin never explained its meaning. … Today Arearea lives in / Musée d'Orsay. / But
  ATUONA knows — / the painting isn't there. / It remains in the gap / between what is / and what the artist decided to see."
- The film opens on a bare spotlit auction-house wall: "What about the painting?" she asked. "Paradise?" (#092). The final shot
  answers it with Arearea on that wall, then the canvas drains to bare while its colour stays as light.

## What they are really testing

They pay you to do three things, and the interview checks all three:
1. **Rebuild a slide so it is clearer**, without losing any fact.
2. **Explain every change in writing**, and also explain what you kept, and why.
3. **Protect accuracy**: terms, caveats, numbers, definitions stay correct.

The sentence to remember: **"I change only what earns it, and I can say why."**

## Your answer shape (every question)

**Point → one real example → why it matters.** Aim for 45–90 seconds. Then stop.

## 2 · Likely questions and answers

**Q: Walk me through how you reconstruct a slide from an image.**
"I work in four steps.
First, I read it. What is the one message? Who is the audience?
Second, I list what must not change: numbers, terms, caveats, footnotes, sources.
Third, I rebuild it in PowerPoint on a clean grid, with the master layout.
I fix hierarchy first: one clear title that says the message.
Then the chart, then spacing and colour.
Fourth, I write the log: every change, and every thing I kept on purpose, with the reason."

**Q: Tell me about a time you kept something instead of changing it.**
"On my investor deck, I added a new slide this month.
I did not change a single existing slide.
They already worked. The story went from problem to proof.
Changing them would have cost consistency and gained nothing.
So my rule is: keep what works, change only what earns it."

**Q: How do you improve data visualisation on a slide?**
"First I ask: what should the reader see in three seconds?
Then I pick the chart for that job.
A trend over time is a line. A comparison is a bar, sorted.
A part of a whole with few parts can be a stacked bar.
I remove what does not help: heavy gridlines, 3D, legends I can replace with direct labels.
I use one accent colour for the point I want them to see. Everything else is grey.
And I never change the numbers, only how they are shown."

**Q: How do you make sure you stay accurate?**
"I come from law. In a contract, one wrong word changes the meaning.
So I copy terms and caveats exactly.
If a slide says 'estimated' or 'as of Q2', that word stays.
If I shorten a sentence, I check the meaning did not move.
In my own publishing system, a number that cannot be traced to a source is not published.
Same rule on a slide: I don't invent, round, or 'clean up' a number."

**Q: What makes an executive slide good?**
"One message per slide.
The title is the conclusion, not the topic.
For example, not 'Audience data', but 'Younger viewers now watch mostly on mobile'.
The body proves the title. Nothing on the slide argues with it.
Executives read the title first. Many read only the title."

**Q: Do you have experience in media or design?**
"Yes, in three ways.
I publish literary work: ninety-eight pieces, so tone and sequence are my craft.
I run a generative film studio, ATUONA. Eight films now, each checked frame by frame.
And I made a product film in nineteen versions.
Most of the gain came from cutting weak shots, not adding more.
That is the same instinct as editing a slide."

**Q: Have you done AI training or evaluation work?**
"Yes. I built an AI judge that scores job postings, with a shared rulebook and automatic tests.
When I replayed fifty-one rejected items, twenty were wrong rejections.
I caught that before release.
I also built a public scoring tool that grades websites on thirty-four checks.
So I know how to write a clear rule, and how to test whether it is applied well."

**Q: Why do you want this role?**
"It uses everything I do best in one task: design, editing, accuracy, and written reasons.
And the reasons are the product here. The AI learns from why I made each choice.
I like work where judgement is the thing being paid for."

**Q: How do you handle the 1–2 hour limit per slide?**
"I timebox. About twenty minutes to read and plan, one hour to build, twenty minutes for the log and a final check.
I fix the big problems first: message, hierarchy, chart.
Polish comes last, only if time allows."

## 4 · PowerPoint features — be ready to name them (and know how to use them)

| Feature | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Slide Master / Layouts | One place to set fonts, colours, placeholders — keeps every slide consistent |
| Guides, grid, Smart Guides | Line things up exactly |
| Align & Distribute | Even spacing between objects |
| Selection Pane | See, name, hide and reorder every object — vital on a busy slide |
| Format Painter | Copy formatting from one object to another |
| Charts → Edit Data | Real editable charts, not pictures of charts |
| SmartArt → Convert to Shapes | Start fast, then edit freely |
| Alt text | Accessibility; describes images and charts |
| Designer | Layout suggestions — use as a starting point, never final |

⚠️ **Honest check before the interview:** if any feature in this table is not something you use
comfortably, open PowerPoint and rebuild one slide using all of them. This role likely has a
practical test. A feature you name must be a feature you can use.

## 5 · Words to be ready to explain (not read aloud)

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Message hierarchy | What the eye should read first, second, third |
| Action title | A slide title that states the conclusion |
| Direct labels | Names written on the chart lines or bars, instead of a legend |
| Chartjunk | Decoration that does not carry data (3D, shadows, heavy grids) |
| Visual cohesion | Every slide looks like it belongs to the same deck |
| Rubric | A scoring guide with clear rules |
| Caveat | A limit on a claim ("estimated", "preliminary", "as of") |

## 6 · Traps

- **Do not claim a design degree or agency job.** You have board-level decks, a live bilingual deck,
  films and publishing. That is enough, and it is true.
- **Do not change a number, ever** — not even to round it. Say this out loud; it is what they fear.
- **Always mention what you KEPT.** The posting asks for it twice (points 4 and 5).
- **Stop talking after the point.** The interviewer is timed; long answers lose marks.
